# 08 · Leisure and games

**Status:** NOT STARTED · **Depends on:** 02, 04 · **Next:** 09

## Goal

Cities build places of leisure that suit the people who live there — an arena, a
hippodrome, a theatre, a tea-house district, a polo maidan, a ball court, a Naadam
ground — pay dearly to build and run them, hold games and festivals that make
heroes, and sometimes regret it.

## Decisions (maintainer)

- Leisure **varies by culture**. Every culture — including **creoles, mixes and
  newly generated cultures** — has **3 preferred leisure types**, and its venues
  and names come from those.
- Venues are **available to every city**; the **council decides by edict** to
  build one (row 04). They are **very expensive**; funding from the treasury,
  **houses, banks, guilds**, or the rich by **liturgy** (any government). Only the
  biggest cities and banks can fund the top tiers.
- Senators have no money of their own for games → they use houses' and guilds'
  money → **houses gain prestige and influence and the senator becomes their
  marionette**.
- **Games happen a few times a year** (from every several months to several per
  year); **a major festival every 3–5 years**.
- **No betting money** — games do not move wealth between houses by gambling.
  (Building, upkeep and sponsorship **do** cost money.)
- An unviable venue is **never converted**: the city regrets it, **looks for more
  financing**, and failing that it declines and is abandoned.
- International games can be held by **any** city with a great enough venue — not
  locked to one culture.
- **Slavery historically accurate**: gladiators from captives only where the
  culture's bondage attitude allows it (row 05); free professionals elsewhere.
- Every venue gets **its own subpanel** with what happens there, its short
  history, and every game and festival held.

## Leisure families (each culture's 3 preferred types)

| Family | Preferred types (3) | Flavour and names |
|---|---|---|
| Greco-Roman-like | Arena · Racing · Theatre (Athletics as alternate) | amphitheatre, circus, odeon; gladiators, charioteers; Olympia-like games |
| Chinese-like | Pleasure district · Ball game (cuju) · Board and mind games | Song *washe* entertainment districts, Tang polo, dragon-boat races, Lantern Festival, Go masters, xiangpu wrestling, cricket fights (no betting money) |
| Steppe-like | Wrestling · Polo/horsemanship · Racing | the Naadam: wrestling, horse racing, archery |
| Persian-like | Polo/horsemanship · Recital · Baths | *chovgan* polo on the royal maidan, poetry recitals, hunts |
| Arabian-like | Recital (poetry fair, like Ukaz) · Racing (horse, camel) · Fair and feast | poetry contests, races, falconry |
| Indian-like | Wrestling (*akhara*) · Board and mind games (*chaturanga*) · Theatre (temple dance, drama) | wrestlers, dancers, chess masters |
| Japanese-like | Wrestling (sumo) · Theatre (noh) · Ball game (kemari) | sumo champions, actors |
| Norse/Germanic-like | Wrestling (glima) · Recital (skalds) · Fair and feast (thing gatherings) | horse fights, skald poetry |
| Celtic-like | Ball game (hurling) · Recital (bards) · Fair and feast (*óenach*, Tailteann games) | hurlers, bards |
| Mesoamerican-like | Ball game (*ulama*) · Fair and feast · River festival | the great ball court, sometimes deadly stakes |
| West African-like | Recital (griots) · Wrestling · Fair and feast (masquerades) | griot epics, wrestling, royal festivals |
| **Slavic-like** | Fair and feast (Kupala and Maslenitsa feasts, fist-fight contests) · Wrestling · Recital (*bylina* singers, *skomorokhi* minstrels) | midsummer fires, winter fist fights on the ice, minstrels and bear-leaders |
| **Egyptian-like** | River festival (the Opet festival, Nile processions) · Board and mind games (*senet*) · Water games (boat jousting) | processions, boat jousting, senet masters, dancers |
| **Southeast-Asian-like** | Water games (boat races) · Theatre (shadow puppetry, *wayang*; court dance) · Wrestling / martial arts (kickboxing contests) | boat races, puppet masters, dancers, fighters |
| Seafaring / Polynesian-like | Water games · Athletics (surfing, swimming) · Fair and feast | canoe races, navigators' feasts |

**How a culture gets its three:**
1. If its **language kit** belongs to a family above → that family's three.
2. Otherwise from **traits**: Martial → Arena/Wrestling · Nomadic/Pastoral →
   Polo/Racing · Scholarly → Theatre/Board games · Artisan → Theatre/Pleasure
   district · Seafaring → Water games · Mercantile → Fair · Agrarian → Fair/River
   festival · Clannish → Wrestling/Recital — top three by weight, ties by a hash.
3. **Creole** (ethnogenesis): the two parents' preferences are merged — the top
   three of the combined weights — so a Greek–Egyptian creole might love Theatre,
   River festival and Racing.
4. **Mixed city**: venue **popularity** = population-weighted share of residents
   whose culture prefers that type; 3 preferences per culture means most cities
   have more than one popular kind.
5. Names come from the culture's naming kit + the venue kind ("the Lantern Court
   of Heshan", "the Great Maidan of Arvan", "the Circus of Kedra").

Every culture always has three — generated worlds never produce a culture without
leisure.

## Building and financing

1. A **major edict** (row 04) proposes a venue: type (weighted by popularity and
   the proposers' ideology) and tier (limited by the Civil level, row 03, and city
   size — tiers 4–5 only for the largest cities).
2. The edict also decides **financing**:

| Source | How | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| City treasury | pays outright | slow, drains other building |
| House sponsor | pays all or part | naming rights ("the Theatre of House Varro"), prestige, influence over senators |
| Bank loan | borrowed by the city | interest; a default can hit the bank (existing bank-failure code) |
| Guild contribution | pays a share | gains a seat voice or a monopoly edict |
| Liturgy | the richest are **obliged** to pay in turn | popular with the commons, resented by the rich; can push houses away |

3. Construction uses real goods (stone, timber) through the same path as row 03
   buildings.

## Running a venue

- **Upkeep** yearly, heavy at high tiers.
- **Ordinary games**: 2–6 a year, each needing a **sponsor**. The official in
  charge (Aedile / Master of the Games, row 04) has no money of his own, so he
  takes a house's or guild's → that house's **control** over him rises (the
  existing bribery code) → he votes as they wish. Lavish games raise the sponsor's
  prestige, the city's prestige and calm unrest; cancelled games raise unrest.
- **Major festival** every 3–5 years: visitors from other cities (a small trade
  bump — the existing fair mechanism), champions made, prestige.
- **International games**: any city with a tier-4+ venue may host periodic games
  open to all cultures; winners bring prestige home; the host can change through
  prestige, war or sack; during the games, participating cities are **less likely
  to war** (the Olympic truce).
- **No betting**: no wealth moves on outcomes.

## Viability, regret and abandonment

A venue whose popularity falls (the population changed, the city shrank) or whose
upkeep cannot be met enters **distress**:
1. the council seeks financing — a loan, a sponsor, a guild, a liturgy (the
   chronicle records it: *"Kedra's council, unable to pay for the Great Arena,
   begged House Varro for aid and was refused"*);
2. failing that, it **declines** (condition falls, games cancelled);
3. finally it is **abandoned** — a ruin that stays on the map and in the history.
   Never converted.

Disasters: collapse (the Fidenae amphitheatre, 27 AD, killed thousands), fire, and
**fan-faction riots** at racing venues (the Nika riots of 532 nearly toppled an
emperor) — factions can align with ideologies (row 06).

## Performers

Performers are `Person`s (row 02) — part of the **40 notables** when famous. Their
lives, traits and events follow row 02; gladiators come from war captives where
bondage is permitted and can win their freedom and fame; a champion can become a
politician.
